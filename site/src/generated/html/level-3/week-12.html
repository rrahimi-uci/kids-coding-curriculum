The big idea: explain the why

Anyone can say what their program does. A real developer can explain why they built it that way. In two minutes, pick one decision and walk through four steps:

Example: “Problem: my first ghost moved randomly and the game was too easy. Choice: I made a Chaser class that is a Ghost, and its move uses breadth-first search with a queue. Why: inheritance meant I only wrote the new part, and BFS always finds the shortest path. Next time: the Chaser is too clever – it should only chase every other turn.”

Good decisions to talk about: why you chose inheritance or composition, why a dictionary or a grid, which algorithm and why, what a test caught, what a playtester taught you.

What next? Keep coding, safely

You can keep learning on your own. Some ideas: give your project a version 2; learn another Python library with a parent’s help; try a new kind of program (a text adventure, a simulation, a puzzle solver); teach someone younger what you know.

SafetyNext steps online

If you want to share your work or join a coding community, choose it together with a parent. Look for clear rules, moderators and a code of conduct. Use a nickname – never your real name, school, age, face or location, in the project or the account. Never move to private chats with strangers, never send photos, and tell an adult straight away if anything feels wrong. Your projects are yours: share only what you want to share.
