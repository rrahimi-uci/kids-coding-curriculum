The big idea: look things up by name

A list finds things by number: backpack[0]. A dictionary finds things by name. Each name is a key, and the thing it gives back is its value. Dictionaries use curly brackets, with a colon between key and value.

A monster as a dictionary

monster = {"name": "Blobby", "health": 30, "attack": 4, "speed": 2}
print(monster["health"])          # 30
monster["health"] = 40            # change a value
monster["color"] = "green"       # add a new key
for key, value in monster.items():
    print(f"{key}: {value}")      # every key with its value

Secret codes: the Caesar cipher

Julius Caesar sent secret messages by moving every letter a few places along the alphabet. With a shift of 3:

plain: a b c d e f g h i j k l m
secret: d e f g h i j k l m n o p
plain: n o p q r s t u v w x y z
secret: q r s t u v w x y z a b c

So hi becomes kl. At the end the alphabet wraps round: x, y, z become a, b, c. To decode, shift the other way. That table is a lookup, so in Python it is a dictionary: {"a": "d", "b": "e", ...}.

TipReal secrets need real protection

A Caesar cipher is easy to crack: there are only 25 shifts to try! Real apps use much stronger encryption. Your real passwords stay between you and your parent or carer. Never type a real password into a program you or a friend wrote.

Cut-out: your Caesar cipher wheel

Cut out both wheels along the thick outer circles. Put the small wheel on top of the big one and push a split pin (or a pencil point) through both centers. Turn the small wheel so that small a sits under big d: that is shift 3. Encode: small wheel → big wheel. Decode: big wheel → small wheel.
